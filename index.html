Results and interpretation

Each bar shows where your score falls. The shaded part is the range the workbook links to autistic traits, starting at its reference point. A score appears once every question in that questionnaire has an answer.

Being in the shaded range is not a diagnosis, and a score is not the chance of being autistic or a measure of severity. The reference points are historical screening cut-offs from the workbook, not diagnostic rules. Many people in the shaded range are not autistic, and some autistic people score outside it.

“High”, “mid” and “low” functioning

You may hear autism described as high-, mid- or low-functioning. These are informal labels, not diagnoses, and these questionnaires cannot tell you which, if any, would apply.

“High-functioning”

Usually used for autistic people without an intellectual disability who speak fluently. It overlaps with what used to be called Asperger syndrome. It can hide real difficulties: many people described this way still need significant support with daily life, work, sensory overload or mental health.

“Mid-functioning”

Has no agreed definition. People use it loosely for someone in between, for example with some language or learning difficulties, or who manages some areas of life independently but needs regular support in others.

“Low-functioning”

Usually used for autistic people with an intellectual disability, little or no spoken language, or high daily support needs. It can underestimate what a person understands and can do, especially people who communicate without speech.

Why clinicians have moved away from these labels

  • Functioning isn’t one number. A person can do very well in one area and need a lot of support in another, and this can change with stress, surroundings or age.
  • They were often based mainly on IQ, which is a poor guide to how someone copes with everyday life.
  • Many autistic people find them inaccurate or hurtful and prefer to describe specific strengths and support needs.

What’s used instead

  • DSM-5-TR rates support needs separately for social communication and for restricted, repetitive behaviours: Level 1 “requiring support”, Level 2 “requiring substantial support” and Level 3 “requiring very substantial support”. It also records whether there is an intellectual or language impairment.
  • ICD-11 records whether there is a disorder of intellectual development and how much functional language is affected.

What this means for your results

Your scores don’t indicate a functioning label or a support level. The AQ and the AAA workbook were designed for adults without an intellectual disability, and a higher score means more traits reported, not more severe autism or greater support needs. Only a clinical assessment can describe someone’s support needs.
